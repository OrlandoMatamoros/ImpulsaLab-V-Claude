## I'm losing money from the register and I don't know where: how to find the leak before blaming an employee

The owner of a bodega in the Bronx counts the cash at closing. The register shows 487 dollars; in the drawer there's 451. Thirty-six dollars difference. Last week it was 28. The week before, 41. No proof of theft, no clear video, just the feeling that something doesn't add up and suspicion falling on whoever works the afternoon shift.

The problem isn't the money that's already gone. It's that we don't know if it's a real hole or a counting error, if it's 36 dollars a day or 12, if the cause is change given wrong or inventory that leaves without being charged. And without that answer, any decision—hiring security, changing staff, buying software—is a shot in the dark.

## What changed in the last two years

- A single shortage of 5 dollars is a counting error; a daily shortage of 20 to 100 dollars is a systemic problem ([dohassist.com](https://www.dohassist.com/resources-blog-why-cash-register-short-every-day)) that can cost thousands of dollars annually if the mechanism isn't detected.

- "Quick-change artists"—customers who pay with a large bill and ask for multiple quick changes while talking non-stop—create confusion so the cashier hands over more cash than corresponds ([apprissretail.com](https://apprissretail.com/blog/getting-to-the-bottom-of-register-discrepancies/)), and the shortage appears at closing without a visible transaction.

- Some point of sale systems register card transactions at the moment of sale, but the money doesn't deposit until the following morning; if you reconcile the register before the bank processes, the register appears short exactly by the amount of pending cards, and the shortage "fixes itself" the next morning ([dohassist.com](https://www.dohassist.com/resources-blog-why-cash-register-short-every-day)).

- Operators who skip daily reconciliation always give the same reasons: lack of time, trust in longtime employees, or belief that small differences don't matter; all three justifications have cost real money to real store owners ([nrsplus.com](https://nrsplus.com/blog/cash-drawer-reconciliation/)).

## How this looks in practice: the three-day method

We start by separating feeling from number. You don't need software, just three elements you probably already have: the cash drawer, the Z report from the register (the summary it prints at the end of the shift with the total sales) and a notebook.

**Day 1: establish the starting point**

Before opening, count the starting cash you load the drawer with. Write it down. Write the name of whoever operates the shift, the start time and that exact amount. At closing, print the Z report. That paper tells you how much should be in the drawer according to the transactions the machine registered. Now count the physical cash, without rushing. Subtract the starting amount: what remains is the real cash from sales. Compare it against the Z report total. The difference—positive or negative—you write down next to the date.

**Day 2 and 3: repeat without changing anything**

Same process. Same shift if possible, or at least same days of the week so volume is comparable. Don't rush, don't give advance notice that you're going to check, don't modify procedures. We want to see the pattern as it's occurring now, not the improved version that appears when everyone knows they're being measured.

By the third day, you have three differences written down. If all three numbers are similar—let's say 28, 36, 31 dollars—the hole is real and systematic. If one day gives +12, another -5 and another +41, the problem is counting or inconsistent procedure, not sustained leakage.

**The calculation that matters**

You take the average of those three days. Let's say it gives 32 dollars daily. Multiply by the days you open per month. If it's a business that operates 26 days, that's 832 dollars monthly. That's not a feeling: it's a measurable expense line, and now you know if it's worth spending 400 dollars on a camera or 150 on changing the cash handling process.

## Where the shortage comes from when it's not theft

Manual counting and giving change are frequent sources of errors; employees can miscount bills, give incorrect change, or rush procedures during peak hours, generating problems that accumulate over time and that without effective controls go undetected until end-of-day reconciliation ([apgsolutions.com](https://apgsolutions.com/cash-handling-best-practices/)).

There are three causes that produce daily shortages without anyone stealing:

**Change miscalculated under pressure**  
A customer pays 17 dollars with a 20 dollar bill. The cashier returns 5 dollars instead of 3. That's two dollars leaving the drawer without being registered in any transaction. If that happens four times in a busy shift, you already have 8 dollars difference with no one's bad intention.

**Product that leaves without going through the register**  
An employee sells a drink to their cousin, collects payment, and puts the cash in the drawer without registering the sale. At day's end, that money is in the register but not in the Z report, so the drawer gives "more" than expected. The opposite case also occurs: the product leaves, the employee forgets to charge or register it, and the drawer comes up short exactly by that amount.

**Discounts applied without documenting**  
The register has a discount or "void" key that cancels a transaction. If an employee uses that function to give someone a special price but doesn't note it, the register expects 15 dollars and only 12 entered the drawer. The difference isn't theft: it's undocumented discretion.

## When NOT to use this measurement method

If your business already reconciles the register at each closing penny by penny, without recurring differences, you don't need this process. You're looking for a problem that doesn't exist.

If you only handle electronic payments—card, Zelle, Venmo—and barely touch cash, it doesn't apply either. The method works for businesses where cash represents at least 40% of daily transactions and where there's enough volume for errors to repeat.

If you already have a point of sale system that forces you to reconcile digitally each shift with individual login per employee, and that system generates an automatic discrepancy report, this manual method is redundant. Use it only if you currently have no way to see that difference day by day.

## What to do when the number confirms there's leakage

You confirmed the shortage is real, sustained and measurable. Now you can decide based on the scale of the problem.

**If the average shortage is less than 15 dollars daily**: review the counting procedure. Require at least two staff members (ideally one manager) to work together counting drawers mid-day as standard process; before opening and at closing, at least two team members record the number of bills and coins in the drawers, noting time and date of count; train cashiers to count each drawer individually and then together out loud, verifying each drawer a minimum of three times ([retailnext.net](https://retailnext.net/blog/how-to-ensure-accurate-precise-cash-handling)).

**If the shortage is between 15 and 50 dollars daily**: the problem is probably in change handling or unregistered transactions. Implement a simple rule: everything that enters or leaves the drawer goes through the register, no exceptions. If an employee needs to give someone a discount, there's a button for that which leaves a trace. If they need to void a sale, they do it with a supervisor present.

**If the shortage exceeds 50 dollars daily**: besides adjusting procedures, consider assigning individual drawers per employee. Depending on the size of the restaurant or business, you can have multiple drawers at any given time; in some places staff share a drawer, but you should avoid that when possible to reduce the possibility of discrepancies and internal theft ([integratedcashlogistics.com](https://integratedcashlogistics.com/cash-handling-procedures-restaurant/)). When each person is responsible for their own drawer from opening to closing of their shift, differences stop being anonymous.

## The most common error: measuring once and acting hot

An owner finds 60 dollars difference on a Friday night. Calls the shift employee, asks what happened, gets no clear answer, and makes a decision—firing, formal warning, new camera—based on a single data point.

The method works only if you meet these three conditions:

1. **You measure at least three consecutive or comparable days** before drawing conclusions. An atypical day (Black Friday, day after changing the price of a popular product, first shift of a new employee) isn't representative.

2. **You don't modify behavior while measuring**. If you announce that this week you're going to check the register, the pattern changes. We want to see how things are occurring now, not how they could occur under supervision.

3. **You separate the number from the person**. The shortage doesn't say who caused it; it says there's a mechanism that allows cash to leave without registration. First you fix the mechanism, then you determine if there was intent.

## What we do at Impulsa Lab when a client needs cash visibility across multiple shifts

We run three types of setup depending on transaction volume and number of employees handling registers.

For businesses with a single register and fewer than three employees, we use Google Sheets with a daily reconciliation template that's filled out by hand in 90 seconds per closing. Costs zero dollars, lives on the owner's phone, and generates a history that can be filtered by employee, day of week or shift. Not sexy, but after 30 days of data you can see if the problem occurs always, only Saturdays, or only when a certain person works.

For businesses with two to four registers, we integrate the point of sale with Airtable or Notion. The system automatically pulls the Z report total (if the POS has an API or exports CSV), and the manager only inputs the physical count from their cell phone. The difference calculates itself, and Airtable sends a notification if it exceeds the threshold you defined. Runs between 15 and 40 dollars monthly depending on the tool, plus the cost of two hours initial configuration.

For operations with five or more registers or franchises, we connect the POS directly with a workflow in Make (formerly Integromat) that crosses three sources: register total, physical count entered by the manager, and video of the closing from the security camera. The flow saves everything in a database, calculates differences by shift and by employee, and generates a weekly report. Costs between 10 and 30 dollars monthly in Make depending on execution volume, plus setup that takes between four and eight hours.

None of these setups require changing point of sale or buying new hardware. They work with what you already have: the register you use today, the phone you count the register from, and the process that already exists but wasn't documented.

## Starting point

Count the register today at closing. Compare it against the Z report. Write down the difference. Repeat tomorrow and the day after. On the third day, multiply the average by the days you open per month: that's the real monthly cost of the leak, if it exists. If the number justifies acting, start with the counting and change-handling process before looking for culprits. If it doesn't justify, you save the notebook and confirm that the problem you felt wasn't as big as it seemed. In both cases, you're no longer deciding blind.
